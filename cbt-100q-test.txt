Human Anatomy: The Upper Limb

The upper limb consists of the shoulder, arm, forearm, wrist, and hand. It is connected to the trunk via the pectoral girdle, which includes the clavicle and scapula.

The shoulder joint is a ball-and-socket joint formed by the head of the humerus and the glenoid cavity of the scapula. It is the most mobile joint in the body but is also prone to dislocation due to its shallow socket.

The brachial plexus is a network of nerves formed by the ventral rami of spinal nerves C5 to T1. It innervates the entire upper limb and gives rise to major nerves including the median, ulnar, radial, axillary, and musculocutaneous nerves.

The biceps brachii muscle flexes the elbow and supinates the forearm. It has two heads: the long head, which originates from the supraglenoid tubercle, and the short head, which originates from the coracoid process of the scapula.

The radial nerve supplies the extensor muscles of the arm and forearm. Damage to the radial nerve, often from a mid-shaft humeral fracture, results in wrist drop due to loss of extensor function.

The carpal tunnel is a narrow passageway on the palmar side of the wrist through which the median nerve and flexor tendons pass. Compression of the median nerve within this tunnel causes carpal tunnel syndrome.

The ulnar nerve passes posterior to the medial epicondyle of the humerus, an area commonly known as the funny bone. Injury here causes tingling and numbness in the ring and little fingers.

Blood supply to the upper limb comes primarily from the subclavian artery, which becomes the axillary artery and then the brachial artery as it travels distally into the arm.
